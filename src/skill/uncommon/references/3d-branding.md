# Optional 3D Branding: A Little Dimension, on Purpose

## When 3D Logo Lab Fits

3D Logo Lab, at https://www.3dlogolab.io/, is an optional browser-based resource for turning a 2D logo into a dimensional presentation. Use it when a user asks for a 3D logo, spinning mark, dimensional wordmark, or a branded 3D asset, or when dimensional branding clearly serves an approved creative direction. It is not a mandatory dependency and does not replace logo design or art direction.

The public site describes uploading a logo, adjusting thickness, color and material, and exporting or embedding the result. Verify the exact inputs, controls, available export formats, account requirements, pricing, embedding terms, and commercial rights in the current interface before promising any of them. The public pages do not establish a documented public API. Do not invent API endpoints, an npm package, automated export scripts, or a working account integration.

## Start With the Mark

If the user has a logo, preserve the approved silhouette, proportions, spacing, and brand recognition. Check that they have the right to use it. For a new logo request, first develop or obtain an approved 2D concept; a 2D-to-3D converter is not evidence of a genuinely original brand identity.

Keep a crisp, accessible SVG or other appropriate 2D original. The dimensional asset is an enhancement for a hero, loading moment, product story, or social asset, not the only usable identity. Test the mark at favicon, navigation, and mobile sizes before adding depth.

## Consent, Access, and Asset Rights

- Do not upload a private, unreleased, or client-owned logo to a third-party service without the user's explicit permission.
- Use only a browser and account the environment legitimately provides. Ask before creating an account, accepting material terms, purchasing a plan, or spending credits.
- Do not bypass a paid export, login, watermark, or access restriction.
- Do not assume public preview materials or examples are licensed for extraction or redistribution.
- Verify current export and commercial-use terms for the actual asset and plan. Retain a provenance record with the source mark, service, date, license, permitted formats, and attribution if required.
- If browser tools, permission, or export access are unavailable, explain the limit and offer a static design direction or an original SVG/CSS/Three.js enhancement instead. Do not claim an export happened when it did not.

## A Practical Workflow

1. Confirm the request and visual goal. Is this a playful clay badge, a polished metal emblem, a translucent product mark, or something else? Choose material to match the brand, not because chrome is available.
2. Obtain or design an authorized, approved 2D source. Simplify unusable tiny details without changing the identity silently.
3. Open the actual 3D Logo Lab interface when browsing tools and consent permit. Inspect available depth, bevel, material, lighting, background, and motion controls.
4. Compare a restrained dimensional treatment with the flat original. Prefer the treatment that preserves recognition, readability, and useful contrast.
5. Export or embed only a format actually offered to the authorized user. Record export settings, access requirements, and rights. Avoid a remote embed when a permitted local asset is lighter and more reliable.
6. Integrate with a static fallback, an accessible name for a meaningful brand mark, reserved dimensions, and a clear pause or reduced-motion state.
7. Test a slow connection, touch interaction, narrow screens, hidden tabs, and browsers without GPU support. Verify that the identity still exists if every effect is disabled.

## Bringing a Dimensional Asset to the Web

Choose the least expensive delivery that preserves the idea. A well-lit still image may be enough. A short muted video can show a turntable without loading a runtime. A real model is appropriate when users benefit from changing the view, a material reacts meaningfully to input, or a character genuinely needs to move in space.

For a live Three.js or React Three Fiber scene:

- Keep models and textures small, cap device pixel ratio, and reuse geometry or materials when appropriate.
- Give every animated property one owner. Drive high-frequency movement with frame callbacks and refs, not React state updates every frame.
- Stop continuous rendering offscreen and when the tab is hidden. Use demand rendering for still and reduced-motion states.
- Dispose scene resources and listeners on unmount. Provide a visual fallback for missing or lost WebGL contexts.
- Keep canvas graphics decorative when adjacent semantic text already names the brand. Do not expose dozens of meaningless mesh labels to screen readers.
- Make an important interaction available through a named HTML button or other native control as well as the scene. Preserve normal scrolling and keyboard navigation.
- Avoid autoplay sound, forceful camera movement, full-screen spinning, or endlessly revolving text. A small deliberate turn usually communicates depth better.

## Characters Are Optional Too

An original 3D character can add warmth, curiosity, and a sense of physical presence when the brief calls for it. Define a recognizable silhouette, a small material palette, consistent lighting, and a few expressive gestures. A blink, weight shift, wave, or reaction to a click is more authored than random perpetual bouncing.

Characters should support the brand and primary action rather than becoming an unrelated game. Keep the first viewport one composition. Do not scatter floating badges, detached controls, or several competing characters across a hero. Match the 3D scene and its still fallback closely enough that capability changes do not break the page.

The uncommon showcase uses original procedural characters, not a claimed 3D Logo Lab export. Do not copy its cozy palette or mascots into every future brief; choose an appropriate world each time.
